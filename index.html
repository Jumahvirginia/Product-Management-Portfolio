Case study

Making stock transfers accountable

An inventory system for a Kenyan wellness and natural-products company (name withheld).

Requirements, testing and rollout support, alongside a freelance developer · About 4 to 6 weeks · Worked with store heads, inventory heads and the developer

At a glance

  • Problem: stock moved between stores on paper, so differences surfaced only at month-end.
  • My part: discovery, a two-way transfer confirmation, testing and training.
  • Result: the company kept using it, and heads reported that month-end reconciliation meetings became much less frequent.

Who did what

I did With the developer The developer did
  • Visited sites, spoke with store heads
  • Reviewed paper records, clarified units
  • Turned findings into requirements
  • Proposed the two-way confirmation
  • Chose what to build first
  • Wrote the test checklist, tested with dummy data
  • Trained staff, gathered feedback, followed up
  • Refined the confirmation approach
  • Investigated bugs and retested fixes

  • Built the software

The wider engagement also included a branch POS and delivery logistics. This case study covers inventory, the main pain point and the one everything else depended on.

The problem

Stock moved from a bulk store, through repackaging, to an inventory store, then to branches and franchisees. All of it was on paper.

  • Units changed at each stage, so counts were hard to match.
  • There was no shared record of sent versus received.
  • Missing or damaged stock went undocumented.
  • Store heads coordinated by text message.
  • Differences piled up until month-end.

Simplified from site visits. Redrawn for this portfolio.

Discovery: my first picture was wrong

  • I visited headquarters and two stores, sat with store heads and read their paper records.
  • I clarified units and packaging with staff, and kept notes and voice recordings.
  • I used AI to tidy rough notes, then reviewed and corrected them. The requirements came from people and records.

I assumed stock went straight to branches and that franchisees bought from branches. In fact, franchisees buy directly from the inventory store, so their purchases are sales with payments, not transfers.

What I first assumed

Stock→Branches→Franchisees

What I found

Bulk store→Repackaging→Inventory store→Branches
↳Inventory store→Franchisees (buy directly)

Red outline: things I did not know before the visits. Redrawn for this portfolio.

Decision 1: confirm transfers from both sides

The insight: recording what was sent does not tell you what arrived.

  • The sender records the dispatch.
  • The receiver confirms what actually arrived.
  • The system shows any difference, and the receiver can record a reason.
  • Unexplained differences stay visible.

I proposed this myself and the developer agreed. I did not take it back to the store heads before building. It makes differences visible. It does not prevent losses.

1 Sender records30 units leave. Sender’s balance reduces.
2 Receiver sees itChecks what physically arrived.
3 Receiver confirms25 units confirmed. Stock reflects 25.
4 System shows the gap5 units: sent does not equal received.
ExplainedMissing, damaged, spoiled or other reason recorded.
UnexplainedStays visible for investigation.

Illustrative example (30 sent, 25 confirmed), not company data.

Decision 2: build inventory first

There were three things to build: inventory, a branch POS and delivery logistics. I started with inventory, because the other two depended on accurate stock.

A gap I found late: franchise credit

  • My first franchise build only recorded payment for what a franchisee took.
  • Before the final draft, I walked the inventory store lead through it in detail. They told me some franchisees buy on credit and pay in installments.
  • We rebuilt around that. Testing then caught a bug: paying in installments did not reduce or show the remaining balance. It was fixed and retested.

Testing before anyone was trained

My bar: core workflows had to work before I trained anyone. I tested with dummy data using my own checklist, then looped with the developer: test, find an error, fix, retest. I did not rank bugs by severity. After the fixes, we tested with staff.

Area What I checked Issue found
Stock movement Outgoing stock reduces. Incoming matches the confirmation Sending store’s stock did not always reduce
Transfer confirmation Receiver sees the transfer, confirms, records a reason Some transfers did not appear at the receiving store
Sync Both sides stay consistent after confirmation One side did not always update
Franchise payments Sale value, partial payments, remaining balance Installments did not reduce the balance

Rollout

  • I trained the store heads first, then the store staff.
  • I had them run real workflows themselves.
  • It went smoothly. Support requests faded after about a week.

What happened

  • The company kept using the system.
  • Differences became visible at transfer time, not at month-end.
  • Two to three months later, store and inventory heads told me month-end reconciliation meetings had become much less frequent.
  • Franchise balances became easier to follow.

Qualitative feedback from the people who used it. I have no measured figures.

What I would do differently

Check with the store heads before building each part. I built the franchise sales flow before learning that franchisees buy on credit and in installments, so I had to go back to the developer and change it. Walking the users through the detail first would have avoided that rework.

What I would measure

  • Share of transfers with a difference
  • Time from dispatch to confirmation
  • Unexplained differences at month-end
  • Hours spent on reconciliation

Measures I would set up, not results I achieved.
